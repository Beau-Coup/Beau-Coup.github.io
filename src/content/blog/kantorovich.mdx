This post covers optimization problems over probability distributions.
It turns out all expected value problems are linear in the distribution, but they are also infinite-dimensional!
To solve the, we need to find a relaxation or a trick that would cast the problem in a finite-dimensional setting.
As we will cover, this is indeed possible by looking at the optimization problem's dual.

## The Kantorovich Problem and its Dual

This post has a lot of definitions. 
While we state everything formally, we do not expect the reader to be familiar with everything. 
Notes permeate this blog to provide a mental model for stated definitions.

First, the problem statement. 
Kantorovich was interested in generalizing Monge's Earth mover's problem.
In essence, how can we transport one mass of objects in a particular configuration to a new configuration with least energy?
Kantorovich formalized it in such a way to allow for mass to split.

<Definition term="Kantorovich Primal Problem">
Fix Polish spaces $X, Y$, marginals $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(X), \nu \in \mathcal{P}(Y)$, and a cost $c: X \times Y \to \R \cup \{+\infty\}$ that is lower semi-continuous.
The *primal* is the following linear program over couplings:
$$\label{eq:primal}
\begin{aligned} 
\inf_{\pi \in \mathcal{P}(X \times Y)} \quad & \int_{X \times Y} c d\pi  \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \pi(A \times Y) = \mu(A) \text{ for all } A \subseteq X \\
                  & \pi(X \times B) = \nu(B) \text{ for all } B \subseteq Y.
\end{aligned} \tag{P}
$$
</Definition>

<Note>
1. Most spaces we will encounter are Polish spaces, Euclidean spaces are Polish spaces.
2. Lower semi-continuous are functions which can be discontinuous. 
However, at each point of discontinuity, the function must take on the lower limit.  
For example, $\mathbf{1}_{(0, 1)}$ is lower-continuous but $\mathbf{1}_{[0, 1]}$ is not.
3. $\mathcal{P}(X)$ denotes the set of probability measures over the set $X$.
4. The conditions on $\pi $ stipulate that the marginals are $\mu $ and $\nu $. 
In words, $\pi $ tells you how mass flows from $\mu $ to $\nu $. 
For $\pi $ to be such a map, all mass in $\mu $ has to end up distributed like $\nu $, and vice-versa.
</Note>

<Definition term="Kantorovich Dual Problem">
The dual to $\eqref{eq:primal}$ is given by 
$$\label{eq:dual}
\sup_{\substack{\varphi \in L^1(\mu), \psi \in L^1(\nu) \\ \varphi(x) + \psi(y) \leq c(x, y) } } \int_X \varphi d\mu + \int_Y \psi d\nu .
\tag{D}
$$
</Definition>

This is a proper dual if we have weak-duality, so let's show that. 
Strong duality requires some additional properties, which we will discuss later.

<Proposition>
Suppose $\varphi \in L^1(\mu), \psi \in L^1(\nu)$.
Further suppose that for the cost $c$ from $\eqref{eq:primal}$ is given, $\varphi(x) + \psi(y) \leq c(x, y)$ for all $x \in X, y \in Y$.
Then,
$$
\int \varphi d\mu + \int \psi d\nu = \int \varphi(x) + \nu(y) \ d\pi \leq \int c(x, y) d\pi.
$$
</Proposition>
