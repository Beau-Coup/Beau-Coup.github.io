Let's just get into it.
Conditional probability usually goes a little something like 
$$
\mathbf{P}(A | B) = \mathbf{P}(A) / \mathbf{P}(B).
$$
That's all fun and good until $\mathbf{P}(B) = 0$.
You might object that there's no point in conditioning if $\mathbf{P}(B) = 0$.
However, there are plenty of cases where we might want to do this. 

 - Case 1. We condition multivariate normal random variables all the time. 
In this case, the probability of observing a particular value $x\in \mathbf{R}$ is $0$.
 - Case 2. Similar to the above, we condition multivariate distributions on individual values, or slices, all the time.

Point is, we should learn what it means to condition a random variable.
Intuitively, conditioning should simply restrict the random variable to the space where the observed variable happened.
But, if the observation has measure zero, so will the conditioned set, so we should probably look at the product space:
$$
Y | X \in B = Y \big|_{\left\{  \omega  \in \Omega \,:\, (\omega, \omega') \in \Omega \times B \right\}}.
$$

Rather than formulate it this way, though, we'll take a turn and define it implicitly. 
In particular, the conditional probability and expectation should always satisfy a marginalization property.

$$
\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{P}(A | X)] = \mathbf{P}(A), \qquad \mathbf{E}[\mathbf{E}(Y | X)] = \mathbf{E}(Y).
$$

Unfortunately, we need stronger conditions for this to actually define the random variables $\mathbf{P}(A | X)$ and $\mathbf{E}[Y | X]$, since infinitely many distributions have the same mean. 
Recall that if $\mathcal{G}$ is a sub-$\sigma $-algebra, then a random variable $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable if $\{Z \leq z\} \in \mathcal{G}$ for all $z \in \mathbf{R}$.
Also $\sigma (X) = \left\{ \left\{  X \in B\right\} \,:\, B \subseteq \mathbf{R} \text{ Borel}\right\}$. 

<Definition term="Conditional Probability and Expectation">
Given random variables $X$ and $Y$ with $\mathbf{E}|Y| < \infty$, 
and an event $A$, $\mathbf{P}(A | X)$ is a *conditional probability* of $A$ given $X$ if it is a $\sigma(X)$-measurable random variable and, for any Borel set $S \subseteq \mathbf{R}$, 
$$
\label{eq:cond-prob}
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{P}(A|X) \mathbf{1}_{X \in S}) = P(A \cap \{X \in S\}).
$$

Similary, $\mathbf{E}(Y|X)$ is a *conditional expectation* of $Y$ given $X$ if it is a $\sigma(X)$-measurable random variable and, for any Borel $S \subseteq \mathbf{R}$, 
$$
\label{eq:cond-exp}
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{E}(Y|X) \mathbf{1}_{X \in S}) = E(Y \mathbf{1}_{X \in S}).
$$
</Definition>

That's nice. And, notice that (3) is a special case of our newest conditions, with $S = \mathbf{R}$.
Now, we'll show that these random variables actually exist. They are only unique, however, up to a set of measure $0$.

<Proposition>
Let $X$ and $Y$ be jointly defined random variables, and let $A$ be an event.
Then $\mathbf{P}(A | X)$ and $E(Y |X)$ exist.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
We may define $\mathbf{P}(A | X)$ as $\frac{\mathrm{d} \nu}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}_0}$ where $\nu$ and $\mathbf{P}_{0}$ are probability distributions defined on $\sigma (X)$ as follows. 
$\mathbf{P}_0$ is simply $\mathbf{P}$ restricted to $\sigma (X)$, and
$$
\nu(E) = \mathbf{P}(A \cap E), \quad E \in \sigma (X).
$$
Note that $\nu \ll \mathbf{P}_0$, so by the general Radon-Nikodym Theorem, $\frac{\mathrm{d} \nu}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}_0}$ exists and is unique up to a set of probability $0$.

Similarly we may defined $\mathbf{E}(Y | X)$ to be $\frac{\mathrm{d} \rho^+}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}_0} - \frac{\mathrm{d} \rho^-}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}_0}$ where 
$$
\rho^+(E) = \mathbf{E}[Y^+ \mathbf{1}_{X \in E}], \qquad \rho^-(E) = \mathbf{E}[Y^- \mathbf{1}_{X \in E}], \qquad E \in \sigma (X).
$$
Then both $\eqref{eq:cond-prob}$ and $\eqref{eq:cond-exp}$ are automatically satisfied, since, for example, 
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{P}(A | X) \mathbf{1}_{X \in S}] &= \mathbf{E} \left[ \dv{\nu}{\mathbf{P}_0} \mathbf{1}_{X \in S} \right] \\ 
&= \int \dv{\nu}{\mathbf{P}_0} \mathbf{1}_{X \in S} \dd \mathbf{P}_0 \\ 
&= \int_{X \in S} \dv{\nu}{\mathbf{P}_0} \dd \mathbf{P}_0 \\ 
&= \nu(\{X \in S\}) = \mathbf{P}(A \cap \{X \in S\}).
\end{aligned}
$$
</Proof>
