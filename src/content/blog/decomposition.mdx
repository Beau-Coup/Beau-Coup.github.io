Basically, every probability measure $\mu$ can be written as the sum of three measures.
One which is completely discrete, one which is absolutely continuous, and one which is singular.

Formally, we have the theorem

<Proposition type="Theorem" term="Lebesgue Decomposition">
Any probability measure $\mu $ on $\mathbf{R}$ can be decomposed as $\mu = \mu_{disc} + \mu_{ac} +\mu_{sing}$ where the measure satisfy

(a) $\mu_{disc}$ is discrete, i.e. $\sum_{x \in \mathbf{R}} \mu_{disc}(\{x\}) = \mu_{disc}(\mathbf{R})$;
(b) $\mu_{ac}$ is absolutely continuous, i.e. $\mu_{ac}(A) = \int_A f d\lambda$ for all Borel sets $A$, for some non-negative, Borel-measurable function $f$, where $\lambda $ is Lebesgue measure;
(c) $\mu_{sing}$ is singular, i.e. $\mu_{sing}\{x\} = 0$ for all $x \in \mathbf{R}$, but there is $S \subseteq \mathbf{R}$ such that $\lambda(S) = 0$ and $\mu_{sing}(S^c) = 0$.

</Proposition>

In other words, measure behave exactly as we intuitively picture them, save the singular measure.
In practice, we rarely encounter singular measures.
Every measure can be represented by a probability density, except for the parts that are discrete, and that's all there is to it.
Another result closes this short note, just to lead into conditional probability and expectation.

<Proposition type="Theorem" term="Radon-Nikodym">
  A Borel probability measure $\mu $ is absolutely continuous if and only if it
  is dominated by $\lambda $.
</Proposition>
