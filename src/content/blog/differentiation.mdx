### The Vitali Covering Lemma 

This is going to be useful, but for now it's just going to be weird.

<Definition term="3 times an interval">
Suppose $I$ is a bounded non-empty interval of $\R$. Then $3 * I$ denotes the interval which has the same center as $I$ and three times the length of $I$.
</Definition>

<Proposition type="lemma" term="Vitali Covering Lemma"> 
Suppose $I_1, \dotsc, I_n$ is a list of bounded non-empty intervals of $\R$.
There exists a disjoint sublist $I_{k_1}, \dotsc I_{k_m}$ such that
$$
I_1 \cup \cdots \cup I_n \subseteq (3 * I_{k_1}) \cup \cdots \cup (3 * I_{k_m}).
$$
</Proposition>

The proof is sessntially a greedy algorithm. 
Start with an empty set.
Take the largest interval that is disjoint from your current set and add it to the set.
Keep doing this until there are no more disjoint intervals.
The proof then shows that $I_j$ must be subsumed in some of those intervals $* 3$, because if not, then we can add it to the set.

### Hardy-Littlewood Maximal Inequality

<Definition term="Hardy-Littlewood Maximal Function">
Suppose $h: \R \to \R $ is a Lebesgue measurable function.
Then the *Hardy-Littlewood maximal function* of $h$ is the function $h^*: \R \to [0, \infty]$ defined by 
$$
h^*(b) = \sup_{t > 0} \frac{1}{2t} \int_{b-t}^{b+t} |h|.
$$
</Definition>

This function gives an upper bound-ish of the density of the function at each point.

<Example>
Consider the function $h = \chi_{[0,1]}$, then 
$$
h^*(b) = \begin{cases} 
\frac{1}{2(1-b)} & \text{ if } b \leq 0, \\
1 & \text{ if } 0 < b < 1, \\
\frac{1}{2b} & \text{ if } b \geq 1. \\
\end{cases}
$$
</Example>

The Markov inequality estimates the size of the set on which a function exceeds a value $c \in \R$.
The next result estimates of the size of the set on which the maximal function $h^*$ exceeds $c$.
It may seem unintuitive or ungrounded for now, but this result will play a central role in the proof of the Lebesgue Differentiation Theorem.

<Proposition term="Hardy-Littlewood Maximal Inequality"> 
Suppose $h \in \mathcal{L}^i(\R)$. Then 
$$
|\{ b \in \R \,:\, h^*(b) > c \} | \leq \frac{3}{c} \| h\|_1, 
$$
for every $c > 0$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Suppose $F$ is a closed bounded subset of $\{b \in \R \,:\, h^*(b) > c\}$. 
We will show that $|F| \leq \frac{3}{c} \int_{-\infty}^\infty |h|$, which implies the desired result.

By definition of $F$, for every $b \in F$ there exists a $t_b > 0$ such that 
$$
\frac{1}{2 t_b} \int_{b - t_b}^{b + t_b} |h| > c.
$$
Clearly, 
$$
F \subseteq \bigcup_{b \in F} (b -t_b, b+t_b), 
$$
and the Heine-Borel theorem tells us that this open cover of a closed bounded set must have a finite subcover.
In other words, there exists $b_1, \dotsc, b_n$ such that 
$$
F \subseteq (b_1 - t_{b_1}, b_1 + t_{b_1}) \cup \cdots \cup (b_n - t_{b_n}, b_n + t_{b_n}).
$$
For convenience, let's denote these by $I_i$
Now, by the Vitali covering lemma, we can further cut this down to disjoint intervals 
$I_{k_1}, \dotsc, I_{k_n}$.
It then follows that 
$$
\begin{align*}
|F| &\leq |I_1 \cup \cdots \cup I_n| \\ 
& \leq |(3 * I_{k_1}) \cup \cdots \cup (3 * I_{k_m}) | \\ 
& \leq | 3 * I_{k_1} | + \cdots + |3 * I_{k_m}| \\ 
& = 3\left( | I_{k_1} | + \cdots + |I_{k_m}| \right) \\ 
& < \frac{3}{c} \left(  \int_{I_{k_1}} |h| + \cdots + \int_{I_{k_m}} |h| \right) \\ 
& \leq \frac{3}{c} \int_{-\infty}^\infty |h|, 
\end{align*}
$$
where the second-to-last inequality comes from our definition of $t_b$.
</Proof>


### Derivatives of Integrals

The next result is the one we've been waiting for. 
It has differntiation in its name, even though no derivative is in sight. 
Don't worry, the derivatives will appear later. 

<Proposition term="Lebesgue Differentiation Theorem v1">
Suppose $f \in \mathcal{L}^1(\R)$. Then 
$$
\lim_{t \downarrow 0} \frac{1}{2t} \int_{b - t}^{b+t} |f - f(b)| = 0
$$
for almost every $b \in \R$.
</Proposition>

The proof is long to type out. 
Basically, the idea is to use Luzin's theoreom, because in the case where $f$ is continuous, the result follows trivially.
We use Luzin's theorem to bound the error, and find that it can only differ on a measure zero set.

<Proposition term="Fundamental Theorem of Calculus"> 
Suppose $f \in \mathcal{L}^1(\R)$. Define $g: \R \to \R$ by 
$$
g(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f.
$$
Suppose $b \in \R$ and $f$ is continuous at $b$, then $g$ is differentiable at $b$ and 
$$
g'(b) = f(b).
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof> 
If $t \neq 0$, then 
$$
\begin{align}
\left| \frac{g(b+t ) - g(b)}{t} - f(b)\right| &= \left| \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{b+t} f - \int_{-\infty}^{b}f}{t} - f(b)\right| \\
                                                &= \left| \frac{\int_b^{b+t}f}{t} - f(b) \right| \\ 
                                                &=  \left| \frac{\int_b^{b+t}(f - f(b))}{t}\right| \\ 
                                                &\leq \sup_{ \{x \in \R \,:\, |x - b| < |t|\} } |f(x) - f(b)|.
\end{align}
$$
If $\varepsilon > 0$, the by the continuity of $f$ at $b$, for small enough $t$ the last quantity is less than $\varepsilon$.
Thus, $g$ is differentiable at $b$ and $g'(b) = f(b)$.
</Proof>
